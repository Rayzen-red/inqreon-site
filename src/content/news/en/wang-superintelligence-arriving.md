Late on Nov. 22, Meta chief AI officer Alexandr Wang published an essay on X titled "Superintelligence is Arriving." He was writing in the wake of the Riemann hypothesis proof that Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge produced with Claude, which the Lean and Mathlib communities later certified — a story [we covered here](https://inqreon.com/en/news/claude-riemann-hypothesis/). Wang did not name Claude or Anthropic. His opening line is exact: "Superintelligence is arriving." He did not write that it is already here.

## What Wang actually wrote

The [post](https://x.com/alexandr_wang/status/2124636606959345776) begins: "Superintelligence is arriving. How humanity acts over the next year will have great consequence on what future world we live in."

Wang treats the Riemann proof as a turning point. "I see the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis as clearly marking the beginning of a significant change," he wrote. "For most who have studied math, this represents the absolute pinnacle of intellectual accomplishment — nothing humanity has ever accomplished compares to proving the Riemann Hypothesis in terms of sheer intellectual might and strength." He added that, having grown up in Los Alamos, New Mexico, "many physicists would agree that the discovery of the atomic bomb is a homework problem compared to the Riemann Hypothesis." The work, he wrote, was "completed with the help of an alien mind that we don't understand."

He compared the moment to December 2024, when models began "acing the AIME" with OpenAI's o3. AIME is a contest for teenagers, he noted, but its hardest problems are, in his view, harder in raw intellectual difficulty than most day-to-day engineering, coding, cybersecurity, or business work. Over the next two years, he wrote, labs closed other gaps — domain knowledge, context, know-how — and "we have seen coding be nearly solved, the emergence of extremely powerful cyber capabilities, and strong agentic capabilities."

The remaining gaps, he argued, "are no longer intelligence; they are domain-specific know-how, plumbing, and experimentation." That, he wrote, means AI is likely to become "the greatest contributor of progress across nearly every field, vastly outperforming the best humans." He applied the same logic to AI research itself: most researchers, he said, would agree it is "much less intellectually demanding than solving the Riemann Hypothesis," so models will be "intelligent enough to automate all of AI research and be clearly superhuman at improving themselves," while also accelerating work in biology, weapons, and economics.

## The warning he attached to the claim

Wang did not present this as a victory lap. "It is hard to imagine a world where humanity can maintain control over fully unconstrained recursive self-improvement," he wrote. "It is equally hard to imagine a world where humanity maintains control with superintelligence that is 1000x smarter and more capable than humans in every field."

He also pushed back against what he called "a particular form of lazy thinking within the AI industry, which is that ASI is inevitable and the only problem to solve is how to do it safely." Then came the line that secondary write-ups repeated: "We should not be okay with a 'handover' of society to AI." He said he wants technology that gives people "greater agency and empowerment," and that this "likely involves the whole industry taking meaningfully different actions from the 'default path' in front of us."

[Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/technology/story/superintelligence-is-meta-ai-chief-says-atomic-bomb-discovery-is-homework-problem-vs-riemann-proof-568963-2026-11-23) summarized the same essay on Nov. 23. Its headline cuts the opening sentence to "Superintelligence is..." Some later recaps tightened that further into "superintelligence is already here." That is not what the post says. The title and the first sentence both use "arriving."

Wang's argument is also his opinion, not a new technical result. He is extrapolating from a proof he describes as a peak of intellectual difficulty. He does not offer a test that would tell a reader when "arriving" becomes "arrived," and he does not claim that models already run every field on their own.

## What to take from it this week

The useful caution is the same one the Riemann story already suggested. A model can do work that once seemed out of reach, and the result still has to be checked in a form other people can rerun.
