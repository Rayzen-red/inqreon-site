This week, reporting based on the Financial Times described KPMG's first AI-based audit of a UK-listed company. In a trial code-named Project Exponential, the firm deployed more than 100 AI agents to review the financial statements of an unnamed FTSE 250 company, running in parallel with a conventional audit by its accountants. Cath Burnet, KPMG's UK head of audit, told the FT that in some situations the agents went deeper and asked "better questions." A person still had to step in, and the experiment was not used when KPMG formally signed off the accounts.

KPMG does not appear to have issued its own press release on the trial. The details below come from the FT report, as carried by The Irish Times, and from IT Pro's follow-up.

## What the trial actually did

According to that reporting, auditors had already used AI for individual tasks, but not across all phases of a large UK audit. In Project Exponential, agents assigned to different parts of the job gathered data, analyzed and documented it, proposed next steps, and drafted conclusions and observations. They also worked with one another.

A team built the agents in four months. KPMG declined to give the exact size of the group, but said two partners and one director worked with senior managers and junior staff to build and run them. An audit partner signed off the experimental audit. That work was not relied on for the formal opinion on the company's accounts.

Human involvement, Burnet said, remained "critical." Staff set the agents' objectives, supplied information about the business and previous audits, checked the work along the way, and challenged the conclusions. In one area, a person had to intervene after an agent failed to match a set of regulations to the company's documents.

Burnet told the FT that KPMG's system provided "full visibility and full traceability" of what the agents did, down to individual characters and timestamps, and that the firm gave the UK Financial Reporting Council a high-level overview of the experiment.

## The limits KPMG is already naming

The firm said there were "clear time-saving opportunities," and declined to say whether that would reduce fees. It plans to expand the project to as many as 20 larger audits and hopes, eventually, to offer AI-based audits to clients. Burnet also said the technology is not yet available at the scale needed to run thousands of audits, that some companies may not want the service, and that few currently have "clean" enough data for the agents to work with.

The same week, the Financial Reporting Council warned that using AI could pose a "distinct threat" to auditors' integrity and independence if they used insights from agents to advise their clients. Auditors are restricted from providing advisory services to audit clients. "Whatever technology a firm uses, the auditor remains accountable," Mark Babington, the FRC's executive director of regulatory standards, said in the same reporting. "Auditors need to be able to explain how they used it, challenge its outputs and stand behind the evidence."

IT Pro, citing the same FT account, also noted that PwC has been running similar parallel trials.
